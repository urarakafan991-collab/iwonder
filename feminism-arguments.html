The arguments against, one at a time

Seven arguments, each taken at its strongest. Where one rests on a factual claim, the claim gets checked.

Same method as the rest of this site. Take the argument as its defenders actually make it, grant whatever can be granted, and see what survives. Where it rests on a factual claim, the claim gets checked, including when the check goes against the side this page is arguing for.

1. Which feminism?

This has to come first, because everything after it depends on the answer.

Feminism isn't one position. Liberal, radical, Marxist and intersectional feminists disagree with each other, sometimes bitterly, and some of the sharpest disagreements are about who counts as a woman at all. There is no membership card and no agreed text. So “feminism says X” is almost always a claim about one strand being charged to all of it.

That cuts in both directions, and this page takes both cuts.

It means a critic can't defeat the whole thing by defeating its worst version. It also means a defender can't claim the whole thing by pointing at its best version, and this page won't try.

What nearly every strand shares is far narrower than the label suggests: that women have been treated as lesser on the basis of sex, that some of that persists, and that it's worth doing something about. That is the claim defended here.

Everything past that core is a live argument among feminists rather than a settled position. If you want to argue against a particular strand, that's a real argument, and it's one feminists are already having with each other.

2. “Women already have equal rights. It's finished.”

Legally, across most of the West, largely yes. That should be said plainly, because it's the strongest version of this argument and it is mostly true.

But look at the shape of what's being said. Voting, owning property in your own name, holding a bank account without a husband's signature, leaving a marriage, not being lawfully rapeable inside one, not being sacked for pregnancy. Every item on that list was refused, argued over, and then granted.

So the argument concedes the case. If feminism is unnecessary now because those things were won, it was necessary then, and it was necessary right up until the moment each one was won. You cannot use the results of a movement as evidence the movement was pointless.

The honest form of this objection isn't “it was never needed”. It's “it succeeded, and what's left is small”. That is a real position, it has evidence behind it, and it's argued properly on the next page rather than dismissed here.

3. “The pay gap is a myth.”

Partly right, and the part it gets right matters enough to say first.

The raw gap is real: in the United States women earn somewhere around 82 cents to the dollar at the median. But that number is constantly presented as women being paid less for the same work, and it is not that. It compares all men to all women across every job, every sector and every number of hours. Anyone using it as a measure of same-job pay discrimination is misusing it, and critics who say so are correct.

Now the part the critics get wrong. Adjusting for occupation, hours and experience shrinks the gap substantially. It does not close it.

And the finding that actually matters is more interesting than either slogan. Claudia Goldin, who won the Nobel for this work, found that most of the gap sits within occupations rather than between them. Equalising men's and women's pay inside each occupation, changing nothing about who works where, would close roughly 68% of it.

Her account of the mechanism is the useful part. Male and female MBAs earn almost identically on graduation. The gap opens after the first child and widens with age. Certain jobs pay disproportionately more for long, rigid, always-available hours, and those hours are far harder to supply while doing most of the childcare. The gap is largest at the top of the distribution, which is the opposite of what you'd expect if it were simply a floor effect.

So the honest version is neither slogan. It isn't “the same job pays women less”. It's that the best-paid work is structured around someone having nobody to go home to, and that someone is usually not the mother.

There is also a real disagreement here that this page will not pretend to settle. Cato reads Goldin's finding that the gap grows with age as evidence that discrimination isn't the main driver: straightforward bias would show up from the start. The Economic Policy Institute points out that controlling for occupation also controls away any discrimination that shaped which occupation a woman entered in the first place, so the adjusted figure is a floor rather than a measurement.

Both are reading the same research competently. That one is genuinely open.

4. “Feminism is about hating men.”

Some feminists have said things that sound like it, and a small number have meant it. That's true of any movement with a century of history, no leadership and no membership requirement.

But the claim is about the movement, not about individuals, and at that level it fails on the simplest available test: look at what was actually demanded.

The vote. Property. Credit and a bank account in her own name. Divorce. Prosecution for rape inside a marriage. Not being fired for being pregnant. Equal pay for identical work.

Not one of those takes anything away from any man, and not one of them is expressible as hatred without changing what it says.

Test it directly rather than trading quotes. Name the demand that requires men to end up worse off. Not something a feminist once said on television. A demand.

5. “It's gone too far. Men are the ones struggling now.”

This is the strongest argument on the page and it gets the numbers rather than a brush-off, because the numbers are real.

In 2023 the US male suicide rate was 22.7 per 100,000 against 5.9 for women, close to four times, with around 39,000 men dead. Men are roughly two in three deaths of despair once overdoses are included. College enrolment runs about 60/40 in women's favour. Sixty-four per cent of students in the bottom tenth of grade point averages are boys, against 34% in the top tenth. Most men today earn less than men did in 1979. The life expectancy gap is approaching six years.

Richard Reeves, who assembled much of this, says it as flatly as it can be said: these problems are not confections of the online manosphere, they're in the data.

And then he says the part that tends to get left out when he's quoted:

“There are still huge issues facing women and girls. If we pose it as a false choice, then we are in trouble.” And: “People can care about more than one thing.”

That's the whole answer. The figures are solid; the inference is the weak link. Nothing in them shows that women's gains produced men's losses. The explanations Reeves himself gives are deindustrialisation, the collapse of wages for men without degrees, and an education system that suits boys less well. None of those is “women did well”.

Notice too what this objection actually is once it's stated carefully. It isn't “feminism's diagnosis was wrong”. It's “there is another serious problem as well”. That is compatible with every word on this page.

6. “Feminists don't care about men's problems.”

As an observation about where attention has gone, this is often fair, and pretending otherwise would be silly.

But a large share of what damages men most is downstream of the same thing feminism identified. Men die by suicide at four times the rate partly because asking for help reads as unmanly, which is a rule about how men are supposed to behave. Fathers get worse custody outcomes partly because of the assumption that mothers are the natural carers, which is the identical assumption that kept women out of paid work. Conscription, workplace death rates, and being told to man up all come off the same template.

You can be angry that feminists have spent most of their time on the women's half of it. That's a complaint about priorities. It isn't an argument that the diagnosis was wrong, and the diagnosis is what's on trial here.

7. “It's anti-family and anti-motherhood.”

Look at what's being objected to. That a woman can leave a marriage. That she owns property inside it. That she cannot be raped in it. That she chooses whether to take paid work.

Every one of those increases the proportion of marriages that are chosen rather than endured. An institution people can leave and stay in anyway is in stronger shape than one they stay in because the exit is closed.

If the objection is instead that fewer women now choose full-time motherhood, that's a claim about what women pick when the choice is genuinely open. It may well be true. It is not an argument for closing the choice.
