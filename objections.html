Common objections

Fourteen objections to the argument, in roughly the order people reach for them.

"Men don't have vaginas. Women don't have penises."

Your own definition produces the thing you say does not exist. Here it is step by step, using nothing but your premises.

  1. A man is an adult male person. Your definition.
  2. Male means a reproductive system organised around sperm production, whether or not it functions. Your definition. The functioning clause is yours too, added to keep infertile men male.
  3. A person with Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome has internal testes. Her reproductive system is organised around sperm production. It produces none, which by your clause does not matter.
  4. So by your definition she is male, and male means man.
  5. She was born with a vagina.

Your definition produces a man with a vagina.

Not a trans woman. Not anyone who chose anything. A person with an XY karyotype whose cells cannot respond to androgens, who developed along the female pathway, was born with a vagina, grew breasts at puberty, was raised as a girl and has lived as a woman her whole life. She is the direct output of the rule you are defending.

You have two respectable options. Accept it: the definition describes reproductive organisation, this is what it produces, and what it produces does not tell you how anyone should be treated. Or reject it: the definition has exceptions, and something other than gametes is doing the work when you call her a woman. What you cannot do is hold both. If the definition settles who is a man, it settles it here. If it does not settle it here, it was never settling it on its own.

"Intersex conditions are disorders within the two sexes. They don't create a third sex."

Agreed, and this site says so on its first page. There is no third sex. CAIS is a condition of male development. The woman with CAIS is male.

Now finish the sentence. She is male, and she is a woman. You have just said both halves. That is the entire argument on this site, and you made it in one breath. "Male" and "woman" are different categories. One does not determine the other. The objection was supposed to defend the link between them, and instead it demonstrates the gap.

"Chromosomes are the real definition. XX and XY don't change."

Chromosomes fail in the same places gametes do, plus a few of their own. Swyer syndrome: XY chromosomes, no functioning gonads, a uterus, a female body, almost always a woman who discovers the karyotype in her teens. XX male syndrome: XX chromosomes, a male body, because a fragment of the SRY gene has translocated onto an X. Mosaicism: different karyotypes in different cells of one person.

There is also a plainer problem. No one has ever checked a karyotype before deciding what to call a person. You did not check anyone's today. Chromosomes have never been the criterion in use. They are the criterion cited once the decision has already been made by looking.

"Sex is observed at birth, not assigned."

Fine. Observed how? By looking at the external genitals of a newborn. That observation classifies the CAIS baby as a girl and the PMDS baby as a boy. So "observed sex" means external anatomy, and external anatomy is the criterion that produced the answers you rejected in the previous two objections when they came out the wrong way. Pick one. If observation at birth settles it, it settles it for her, and she is a woman.

"You admit sex is binary, then you talk like it's a spectrum."

Nobody here said spectrum. The gamete definition is binary and this site holds it. What varies is the set of markers people use to guess at sex without measuring gametes: chromosomes, hormones, anatomy, presentation. Those markers do not always agree, which is what intersex conditions are. That is "the proxies are imperfect," which is a different claim from "sex is a spectrum," and only one of them is being made.

"There's no such thing as gender identity. The brain studies are junk."

The brain studies are weaker than advocates claim, and this site says so. The imaging findings are population averages with small effects, several are confounded by sexual orientation, and none of them is diagnostic of anything.

It does not matter. Gender dysphoria is diagnosed the way every psychiatric condition is diagnosed, on symptoms, course and impairment, and nobody withholds treatment for depression or OCD because no scan can find them. The distress is real, it is predictable, it does not respond to being told to stop, and it responds to a specific treatment. Whether the word "identity" names something you find philosophically tidy is not the question. The question is whether a person with this diagnosis is owed what everyone else with a diagnosis is owed.

"People just prefer to present as the other sex. It's a choice."

"Prefer" is doing all the work and it has not been earned. If it is a preference, defend the word. Explain why someone would choose the thing that gets them disowned, harassed, assaulted and unemployed at rates that nobody prefers. Explain why the preference persists through every incentive to drop it. Explain why people describe it from early childhood, before they had any idea what it would cost them.

And notice that if it were a preference, it would be reversible by choosing again. Regret after gender-affirming surgery in adults runs at about 1% in the largest pooled review, 27 studies and nearly 8,000 patients, and the caveat that instruments varied and follow-up was incomplete does not get you anywhere near a preference people abandon.

"Having a medical condition doesn't mean anything can be anything."

Nobody said it does. A dog with a condition that stops it barking is still a dog, because barking was never the criterion. A woman with a condition that stops her producing eggs is still a woman, because egg production was never the criterion either, as the previous objections established. That is not self-declaration. It is what you already believe about the woman with CAIS.

The definition on this site is not "anyone who says so." It requires a diagnosis and medical transition, undertaken or intended. That is a narrower gate than the one you would accept for any other condition, where a diagnosis alone is enough for everyone to adjust.

"A physical anomaly is easier to accommodate than a neurological one."

"Easier" is not a criterion for whether accommodation is owed. It is a statement that you would rather not. Neurological and psychiatric conditions are accommodated everywhere: epilepsy, autism, ADHD, PTSD, dementia. Difficulty has never been the standard, and a person who reaches for it has run out of the other kind of argument.

"Just treat the condition medically. Why does society have to rearrange?"

Transition is the medical treatment, and it works partly because it is social. A treatment whose purpose is to let someone live as a woman does not work if everyone around her refuses to let her. Nobody is asking society to rearrange itself. Using a name and a pronoun and not making a scene is the courtesy already extended to every other treated condition, at no cost to anyone.

"Treatment is a choice. They chose to transition."

All treatment is a choice in the sense that you can refuse it and suffer instead. A diabetic chooses insulin. A person with a shattered leg chooses surgery. A person with cataracts chooses the operation. We do not withhold accommodation on the ground that the treatment was decided on, because the entire concept of medical accommodation rests on the opposite: when someone has a condition they did not pick and treats it, the treatment is accommodated, because the alternative is pointless suffering.

You would not tell a cancer patient that because chemotherapy was a choice, accommodating the hair loss is optional. You would not tell someone with a prosthetic leg that because the surgery was elective, treating them as someone who walks is a favour.

"It's just biology, accept it" is the same move. Biology is corrected by medicine every day and the corrections are accommodated without anyone objecting. Glasses correct the biology of poor eyesight and nobody tells the wearer to accept the world as a blur. Hearing aids, pacemakers, insulin, prosthetics. That line is not deployed against any of them. It is deployed against one treatment, and the only thing that distinguishes that treatment from the rest is that it bothers the person deploying the line.

"Most kids grow out of it. It's social contagion."

Two claims, both about children, and the argument on this site is about adults. Take them anyway.

On desistance: the older follow-up studies found high rates of children who stopped identifying as the other sex, and they used broad criteria that would now be considered not to identify gender dysphoria at all. More recent cohorts using DSM-5 criteria find much lower rates. It is contested, and the honest answer is that the number depends on who was counted.

On contagion: the rise in referrals is real and unexplained. The "rapid onset" hypothesis came from a survey of parents recruited on forums opposed to transition, has not been validated in any clinical sample, and is not a diagnosis anywhere. Both sides overclaim here and this site will not.

Neither claim reaches an adult with a diagnosis who has transitioned, which is the person the definition here describes.

"The Cass Review proved the evidence is weak."

For adolescents, the systematic reviews commissioned for Cass found the evidence on puberty blockers insufficient and on hormones in under-18s low quality, and this site says so on its first page rather than pretending otherwise.

Cass reviewed services for children and young people. It said nothing about adults. It did not question whether gender dysphoria exists, did not recommend ending treatment, and recommended that puberty blockers be provided within a research framework rather than not at all. Anyone citing it as evidence that adult transition is unsupported is citing a document that does not contain that finding, and usually has not read it.

"Women's spaces exist because of male violence. A male is a male."

This is the real disagreement, and it is a policy question rather than a definitional one. It turns on evidence about effects in specific places, and the evidence is thin, because no country records the sex, gender and transition status of offenders in a way that answers it. Both sides are currently arguing from anecdote.

Notice what the objection assumes. It assumes that the answer to "what is this person called" and the answer to "who should be in this prison wing" are the same question. They are not, and every other category in life shows it. We do not decide what to call someone by first deciding which spaces they may enter. If you think trans women should not be housed in women's prisons, argue it on the evidence about prisons. That argument does not require you to call anyone a man.

"Male puberty gives advantages that hormones don't reverse. Sport is unfair."

Largely true. Height, skeletal structure and lung capacity do not reverse with hormone therapy, and this site does not claim they do. Elite sport is a downstream policy question, it should be decided on measurements, and the answer may well be sex-based categories with an open category alongside.

What that does not do is reach a single other domain. A person who thinks a trans woman should not compete in the women's 100 metres can still call her a woman, and most people who follow sport already do exactly that with no strain at all. The sport argument is an argument about sport. It gets used as an argument about everything, and it has no reach beyond the track.
