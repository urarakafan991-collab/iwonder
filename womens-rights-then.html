The arguments that were made

Six arguments, as their defenders actually put them, and what happened to each one.

These are not strawmen. Every argument below was mainstream, published, and defended by educated people who considered themselves reasonable. Several carried the authority of the science of their day. They are given here roughly as their defenders put them.

1. “Education will damage them.”

In 1873 a Harvard medical professor, Edward Clarke, published Sex in Education; or, A Fair Chance for Girls. His argument was physiological: the body has a limited budget of energy, and sustained study would divert it to the brain at the expense of everything else. His specific prediction was that if women went to college their brains would grow larger and heavier and their wombs would atrophy.

The book sold out in a week.

This was not a crank pamphlet. It was a Harvard professor, writing as a physician, reviewed seriously, and widely believed.

Women have since attended university in enormous numbers for a century and a half.

The predicted effect did not occur in anyone. It is about as cleanly falsified as a claim about human beings can get.

2. “All government rests on force, and women cannot supply it.”

This was the argument anti-suffragists themselves considered strongest, and it appeared in their published lists in almost those words: that all government rests ultimately on force, to which women, for physical, moral and social reasons, are not capable of contributing; and that women cannot hold full citizenship because they are not available for national defence.

It deserves to be taken seriously, because its premise is partly true. Laws are backed, in the last instance, by coercion.

The conclusion still doesn't follow, and the way to show it is the same test this site uses everywhere: apply the rule consistently and see what it produces.

Applied evenly, it disenfranchises every man too old to fight, every man too ill, every man with a disability, every man in a reserved occupation. Nobody proposed that. The criterion was never once used to remove a vote from a man who couldn't supply force.

A rule applied to one group and not to another isn't a rule. It's a conclusion with a rule attached afterwards.

3. “Women are already represented by their husbands.”

Set this next to the other argument the same people made: that enfranchising women would set spouses against each other and damage the peace of the family.

These cannot both be true.

If a wife's interests are already fully expressed by her husband's vote, then her own vote changes no outcome and no household is disturbed by it. If her vote would produce conflict, then her interests were not being represented, and the first argument was false.

Both were deployed, often by the same organisations, sometimes on the same page. When two arguments that contradict each other are used to defend one conclusion, neither of them is what's holding the conclusion up.

4. “Most women don't even want it.”

Partly true, and worth saying so rather than skipping past it. There were organised women's anti-suffrage groups. Mrs Humphry Ward founded the National Anti-Suffrage League in 1908. Queen Victoria had called the idea wicked folly.

But look at the shape of the argument. It withholds a right from a group on the grounds that not all of them have demanded it. That reasoning has never been applied to any other right, and it cannot be, because it makes every right conditional on unanimous petition from the people who don't have it.

It also settled itself. Once women had the vote, they used it, and the anti-suffrage organisations dissolved rather than campaigning for repeal.

5. “Their physiology makes them unfit.”

This was argued with the full authority of medicine. Menstruation and menopause were offered as evidence that women were too irrational to be trusted with a vote. Contemporary metabolic theory was used to argue that women were constitutionally conserving rather than expending of energy, and therefore lacked what public life required.

In March 1912 Sir Almroth Wright, a distinguished bacteriologist, published a letter in The Times arguing that women were generally prone to hysteria in a way that made them unfit to receive the franchise.

The medical profession broadly supported these positions, and lent them scientific authority, while knowing very little about female physiology.

This is the one worth sitting with. None of it was presented as prejudice. It was presented as science, by scientists, in the most respectable outlets available.

Confidence and credentials are not the same thing as being right. That hasn't changed since 1912, and it's worth remembering whenever a current claim arrives wearing the same clothes.

6. “Separate spheres.”

The umbrella the rest sheltered under. Men and women were held to be ordained, by God or by nature, for different domains: the public world for men, the domestic one for women, each supreme in their own, and crossing between them destructive to both.

Note the escape hatch built into it. Women were described as superior within their sphere, which let the arrangement be presented as honour rather than exclusion, and let its defenders feel generous while defending it.

There's a simple test for whether that was meant. The superior sphere came with no vote, no property in your own name, no wages you could keep, and no way out of a marriage.

A hierarchy described as a partnership is still a hierarchy when only one side is able to leave.

7. What all six have in common

Every one of them was respectable. Every one was held by intelligent people who had thought about it. Several came with the science of the day attached, argued by professors and published in the best papers.

And every one of them is now dead. Not unfashionable. Dead. There is no organised movement to repeal women's suffrage, no serious literature arguing that university study atrophies the womb, nobody writing to The Times about hysteria and the franchise.

They didn't lose because their opponents shouted louder. They lost because each one was tested against what actually happened, and none of them survived it.

That's the track record. It's the reason the arguments still being made get examined on the next page rather than waved away, and also the reason they don't get the benefit of the doubt just for sounding reasonable. So did these.
