The arguments, one at a time

Six arguments, each taken at its strongest. Where one rests on a factual claim, the claim gets checked.

Same method as the rest of this site. Take each argument the way its defenders actually make it, grant whatever can be granted, and see whether it survives. Where an argument rests on a factual claim, the claim gets checked. Where it rests on a principle, the principle gets applied consistently to see whether anyone is willing to live with the result.

1. “It's a choice.”

This is the only item on the list that has been tested directly, at scale, by people who badly wanted it to come out the other way.

If orientation were chosen, it would be changeable. For decades that was attempted deliberately, through what the literature calls sexual orientation change efforts: clinical and religious programmes, substantial funding, and participants who volunteered because they wanted it to work.

It didn't. The American Psychological Association's 2009 task force reviewed the research and found the evidence insufficient and of low quality, with no reliable change in orientation. A 2015 survey of 1,612 people who had been through these programmes found that 3.2% reported even slight change.

Notice what makes that number so damaging to the argument. The sample was selected for motivation. These were not people being talked out of anything. They were people who wanted to change, often at enormous personal cost, and it still didn't happen.

It also caused harm. A 2020 study found that young people whose parents attempted to change their orientation reported around three times the likelihood of high depression levels, suicide attempts, lower educational attainment and lower income in adulthood. In 2021 the APA passed resolutions opposing these efforts outright.

The strongest possible test of “it's a choice” was run for decades, with willing participants, by people who wanted it to be true.

It failed. You don't need a theory of where orientation comes from to notice that.

There's a smaller point worth making too. The choice claim has a consequence its holders never accept: that they chose as well. Ask someone who says this when they decided to be straight. The question isn't a gotcha. It's the argument applied to the person making it.

2. “It's unnatural.”

Two separate problems, and the second matters more.

Factually, the claim is false. Same-sex sexual behaviour has been recorded in over 1,500 animal species, spread across most major branches of the animal kingdom. A 2009 review in Trends in Ecology & Evolution catalogued many thousands of documented instances of same-sex courtship, pair bonding and copulation across mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, insects, molluscs and nematodes, and noted that these are probably undercounts, partly because researchers tend to assume a pair engaged in sexual behaviour must be male and female.

But suppose none of that were true. The argument would still fail, because “natural” was never the criterion and nobody applies it.

Glasses are unnatural. So are antibiotics, caesarean sections, chemotherapy, vaccination, insulin, air travel, cooked food and reading. Meanwhile infanticide, parasitism and forced copulation are entirely natural and occur throughout the animal world. If natural meant good and unnatural meant wrong, the list of things you'd have to give up and the list of things you'd have to accept would both be unbearable.

Used as a real criterion, “unnatural” forbids your medicine and permits things you'd call monstrous.

Nobody uses it as a criterion. It's a word applied afterwards to a conclusion already reached by other means.

3. “There's no gay gene, so it isn't innate.”

True, and irrelevant, in that order.

The largest study of this to date analysed 477,522 people and was published in Science in 2019. It found five genetic locations significantly associated with same-sex sexual behaviour, and calculated that all measured genetic variants together account for somewhere between 8 and 25% of the variation. No single gene. No ability to predict any individual.

So yes. There is no gay gene.

There's no gene for height either. None for intelligence, or for handedness, or for almost any human characteristic anyone cares about. Polygenic, partly heritable, interacting with development and environment is what nearly every interesting trait looks like when you go and measure it. “Not one gene” is the ordinary result, not a surprising one.

And look at what the argument needs in the middle. To get from “no single gene” to “therefore chosen” you need the premise that anything not reducible to one gene must be voluntary. Nobody believes that. It would make handedness a lifestyle.

Being accurate about this cuts both ways, and the honest version is still enough. This research does not prove “born this way” in the strong sense people sometimes want it to. What it shows is a real, partial, heritable contribution to a complex trait. Put that next to section 1 and the position is: there is a biological component, there is no evidence of choice, and the most determined attempt in history to change it produced nothing.

4. “Children need a mother and a father.”

This is an empirical claim, which means it can simply be checked.

Reviews of the research find a clear consensus: children raised by same-sex parents do comparably to children raised by different-sex parents across academic performance, cognitive development, social development, psychological health, early sexual activity and substance use.

There is one widely cited study pointing the other way, Regnerus in 2012, and it's worth knowing exactly why it doesn't carry the weight put on it. Reanalyses of his own data found the results fragile and largely produced by misclassification and other analytical choices. One concluded that the analysis failed to control for family instability during childhood, and that once it did, same-sex parenting was weakly or not at all associated with poor adult outcomes. The American Sociological Association, the American Academy of Pediatrics and the American Medical Association all dispute its conclusions.

The clearest problem, though, is in the study's own numbers. Of the 248 children it counted as having a gay parent, two had lived with that parent and their partner for the whole of their childhood. Two.

That figure does not come from a critic. It comes from an organisation defending the study.

So the comparison being run was not “two mothers against a mother and a father”. It was children of intact families against children of families that fell apart, with a label attached to one group.

That's a finding about instability. It was reported as a finding about gay people.

5. “My religion says it's wrong.”

This one is different in kind and deserves to be treated that way. A religious conviction isn't an empirical claim, and there's no sense pretending a study refutes it. Two things follow anyway.

The first is consistency. Where a specific scriptural prohibition gets cited, it usually sits in a passage surrounded by prohibitions that nobody present enforces, about fabric, food, lending and much else. If the text binds here but not three verses later, something other than the text is deciding which lines carry forward. That is the same problem this site raises everywhere else: when a rule returns different answers for equivalent inputs, the rule is not what's producing the answer.

The second is more decisive, and it concedes more. Suppose the conviction is entirely sincere and internally consistent. It still binds the person who holds it. It is an argument for not entering a same-sex marriage yourself.

Getting from there to a law, a dismissal, or a refusal of service needs one more premise: that your religion should govern people who do not share it. That premise isn't a religious claim and scripture doesn't supply it. It has to be argued for separately, on grounds everyone can examine.

6. “It's a threat to society.”

Take the strongest version rather than the loosest: fewer people in opposite-sex couples means fewer children, and societies need children.

It doesn't work, because the arithmetic assumes a substitution that doesn't happen. Gay people prevented from having same-sex relationships do not convert into people who want opposite-sex ones. What the historical alternative actually produced was not more children. It was closeted marriages that ended badly, and that outcome was worse for everyone involved, the children included.

The birthrate version doesn't survive contact with the data either. The countries with the lowest birthrates are not the most accepting ones, and acceptance and fertility vary independently across countries that have plenty of both.

The “threat to the family” version is stranger still once you say it plainly. The thing being objected to is people wanting to marry each other, commit to each other, and in many cases raise children together. If the family is the value being defended, this is a peculiar place to aim.
