Common objections

The moves that come after the first answers have landed.

The arguments page answers the case as it's first made. These are the moves that come next, once the first answers have landed.

“I'm not homophobic. I have gay friends.”

Probably true, and not a response to anything.

The question on the table was whether the position holds, not whether you're a bad person. Nobody has said you are. If the argument is sound it survives without the character reference, and if it isn't, the friends don't rescue it.

“I tolerate it. I just don't have to celebrate it.”

Agreed, and nobody is asking you to.

But check what “celebrate” is standing in for. In practice it nearly always means: not being fired, not being refused service, being allowed to marry, and existing in public without comment.

Those aren't celebration. They're the floor you already stand on without noticing it. If the only things you're objecting to are things you have yourself, the word for what you want isn't tolerance.

“Why does it have to be so public?”

Worth noticing what registers as public.

A man mentioning his husband is public. A man mentioning his wife is conversation. A photograph on a desk is a statement in one case and furniture in the other. Holding hands is an act in one case and nothing at all in the other.

The asymmetry is the whole answer. Visibility only reads as insistence when it's unfamiliar. And the reason it was ever hidden was never modesty; it was that being seen was dangerous, and in much of the world still is.

“Don't teach it to children.”

Depends entirely on what's meant, and the two meanings get deliberately blurred.

If it means explicit sexual content: agreed, and that isn't what's happening. If it means a child knowing that some families have two dads, that isn't sex education. It's a fact about the world, and children already know it, because they have met those families.

The objection is also never applied evenly. A picture book in which a prince marries a princess has never once been described as teaching children about sex.

“Marriage has always meant one man and one woman.”

It hasn't. Polygamy is extensively documented across societies and eras, and what marriage requires, permits and means has been rewritten repeatedly, including very recently.

But suppose it had always meant that. “It has always been this way” is a description, not an argument. It was true of every extension of marriage that has since happened, including to interracial couples, and it was exactly as true and exactly as irrelevant then.

“What about the baker? Doesn't religious freedom count?”

It does, and this is the one item here with a real competing interest rather than a mistake in it, so it should be said plainly: that is a genuine conflict of rights, and reasonable people land in different places on where commercial service ends and compelled expression begins.

What it is not is an argument about whether gay people should exist, or marry, or be employed. It is a narrow question about the reach of public accommodation law.

Using the hardest edge case to settle the easiest central one is the move being made here. If the central question were genuinely resolved in your favour, you wouldn't need the baker.

“They voted homosexuality out of the manual. That's politics, not science.”

Every category in that manual is decided by a committee, because that is what a diagnostic manual is. If a vote invalidates a diagnosis, it invalidates all of them.

What matters is the reasoning. The criterion used was distress or functional impairment, which is the same criterion applied to everything else in the book, and applied consistently it did not fit. The World Health Organization reviewed it separately and reached the same conclusion in 1992.

“You keep comparing it to race and that's offensive.”

Two different things get called a comparison, and only one of them is being made.

Saying that gay people and Black people are in the same situation would be false, and it isn't claimed anywhere here. Saying that a particular argument has been made before, in the same shape, about a different group is a claim about the argument rather than about either group.

“It's unnatural.” “It will harm children.” “It undermines marriage.” “It's against scripture.” All four were used against interracial marriage, in those words.

That isn't equating the experiences. It's noticing that a form of argument has a track record, and the track record is bad.

“I just find it disgusting.”

This is the most honest thing on the page, and it deserves more respect than the arguments built to avoid saying it.

Disgust is real, it isn't chosen, and being told not to feel it doesn't work. But it isn't evidence about anything outside the person feeling it. People are disgusted by foods, by surgery, by insects, by other people's marriages. None of the others is treated as grounds for a law.

The test is quick. Would you accept “I find it disgusting” from someone else, about something you do, as a reason to restrict you? If not, it isn't carrying the weight you need it to carry.
