If you believe sex determines gender

Taking the gamete definition seriously and applying it consistently.

The belief this page takes as given

A woman is an adult female person. A man is an adult male person.

Female means someone whose reproductive system is organised around the production of eggs. Male means someone whose reproductive system is organised around the production of sperm. Whether it functions is beside the point; an infertile man is still male.

Conceded before we start. Sex is binary. There are two gametes and no third. Intersex conditions are variations within male or female development, not a third sex. A trans woman is male by this definition. None of that is disputed anywhere on this site, and if you came looking for an argument that sex is a spectrum you will not find one.

The concession is deliberate. It leaves you holding the definition, and the definition has consequences you have not followed through.

1. What the definition produces

A person with Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome has an XY karyotype and internal testes. Her cells cannot respond to androgens at all, so she developed along the female pathway in the womb. She was born with a vagina, was raised as a girl, went through a female puberty of breast development and a female body shape, and usually learns of the condition in her teens when periods do not arrive. Some never learn of it. She has lived as a woman every day of her life.

Run her through the definition. Her reproductive system is organised around sperm production, and by your own clause it does not matter that it produces none. She is male. Male means man. Your definition says she is a man.

She is not an edge case to set aside. She is the direct output of the rule. So there are three things you can say next, and each of them ends the argument in the same place.

If you say she is a woman

Then the definition has an exception you did not declare, and something other than gamete organisation produced your answer. Whatever that something is, it is the real criterion, and you have just used it to overrule the one you said was decisive. The only question left is why it applies to her and not to a trans woman, when the definition returns the same result for both.

If you say she is a man

Then "man", as you use it, includes a person with a vagina, breasts, no penis, no male puberty and no day lived as a man. That is a coherent thing to say. It also empties the word of everything you wanted it to do, because her classification changes nothing about how she is treated, where she goes, or what anyone calls her. If "man" can include her and change nothing, it can include a trans woman and change nothing.

If you say she is a woman because she has a medical condition

Then your criterion is now: biologically male, but living as a woman because of a condition that was not chosen. Nobody with Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome chose to have it. Nobody with gender dysphoria chose to have it either. That is the same criterion applied to two different people, and it produces the same answer for both. You have arrived at the definition on the definitions page, and the rest of this site is the working.

Every route out of the CAIS case concedes that gamete organisation does not, on its own, decide what someone is called or how they are treated. It decides what their reproductive system is organised around. Those were always two different questions, and the argument only works while they are kept blurred.

2. It fails in the other direction too

Persistent Müllerian Duct Syndrome is a condition in which a 46,XY person, male by every clause of the definition, is born with a uterus and fallopian tubes because the body did not respond to Anti-Müllerian Hormone. The testes are usually present and the external anatomy is male. Almost everyone with the condition identifies and lives as a man, and most are diagnosed incidentally, during surgery for something else. The retained uterus is usually rudimentary. In some documented cases it has enough function to shed its lining.

So the framework contains men with a uterus, and occasionally one that bleeds on a cycle. Nobody calls them women. Nobody proposes to.

3. Watch what actually decides the answer

Put the cases side by side and look at which criterion is producing each verdict.

The woman with CAIS is male by gametes and gonads, female by external anatomy and by every day of her life. She is called a woman. The man with PMDS is male by gametes and gonads and external anatomy, and has a uterus. He is called a man. The trans woman is male by gametes and gonads, has female external anatomy if she has had surgery, and has lived as a woman for however long she has. She is called a man.

For the first two, anatomy and life history decide. For the third, gonads decide. The criterion switches to whichever one produces the answer the speaker wanted before the case came up. A rule that returns different outputs for the same input is not the rule being applied. It is the rule being cited.

4. Every criterion on offer breaks on someone

When gametes give the wrong answer people move to a different marker. Each one fails on a real person, and the failures are not obscure.

  • Gametes actually produced. Fails on every infertile adult, every post-menopausal woman and every child. Nobody thinks a nine-year-old girl is not a girl because she produces no eggs. Abandoned as soon as it is stated.
  • What the system is organised around. Fails on CAIS, as above. Also on Swyer syndrome: XY, no functioning gonads at all, a uterus, a female body, usually a woman who finds out at puberty. Her system is organised around nothing.
  • Chromosomes. Fails on Swyer (XY, uterus, woman), on XX male syndrome (an SRY fragment on an X chromosome produces a male body with XX chromosomes), and on mosaicism. It also has never once been the operating criterion for anyone, because nobody has checked a karyotype before deciding what to call a person. It is produced after the decision, as the justification.
  • Genitals at birth. Classifies the CAIS baby as a girl. That is the answer you rejected two bullets ago when it came from gametes. It also tells you nothing about the adult in front of you, whose genitals you have not seen and are not going to see.
  • Current genitals. Puts a trans woman who has had surgery in the women's category and a trans man who has not in the same category, with his beard and his voice. Nobody who proposes this rule wants what it produces.
  • The birth certificate. Fails on every jurisdiction that amends them, which is most of them, and reduces the argument to a filing system.
  • "You can just tell." Fails on every trans person who passes and every cis woman who is stopped at a bathroom door for being tall and short-haired, which happens. It is also a concession that the real criterion is presentation. Presentation is the thing trans people change.

None of these is applied consistently by the people who cite them, and the reason is simple. None of them is what is being tracked. What is being tracked is the social category, decided first, and the biology is named afterward to dress the decision.

5. Typical traits are not definitional requirements

The usual reply at this point is the two-legs argument. Some people have one leg; that does not mean humans do not have two legs. So some women do not produce eggs; that does not mean women are not defined by eggs.

Look at what the argument requires. In biology nobody says humans strictly have two legs. The claim is that humans typically have two legs, and a person with one is a human with one leg. Apply that same word, typically, to sex and there is no disagreement here at all. Women typically have reproductive systems organised around eggs. Men typically have systems organised around sperm. Not all do. That is the whole of this site's position on the biology.

The disagreement is about the word strictly. If a woman is strictly and without exception someone whose system is organised around eggs, then the woman with CAIS is strictly a man, and you are back in section 1. If you will not say that about her, you are not using strictly, and the trans woman is not excluded by a rule you have stopped applying.

6. Why the definition behaves this way

None of this makes the biological definition wrong. It makes it a tool being used for a job it was not built for.

The gamete definition is a classification of reproductive roles. It sorts a species into the smallest number of categories that account for how it reproduces, and for that purpose two categories do the work. It has been refined every time a new condition was described, which is how biology treats every category it has. It says nothing about what anyone should be called, where they should go, or what courtesy they are owed, because it was never asked to.

The definition tells you what a reproductive system is organised around. It does not tell you who someone is socially or how they should be treated. It was never doing that job, and the CAIS case is where you can see it not doing it.

7. Why it generates no social rules

Every social rule built around sex tracks something downstream of the definition. Bathrooms are separated by convention, appearance and the expectation of privacy, not by gamete organisation, which nobody in a bathroom can observe. Sport is separated on hormones, muscle, skeleton and lung capacity, which is why the debate about trans women in sport is a debate about those things and not about chromosomes. Prisons are separated on risk. Changing rooms on undress.

Ask which marker should govern each of these and the proposals collapse in the same way the criteria did. Chromosomes put the woman with CAIS in the men's room and require a test nobody administers. Current genitals put a bearded trans man in the women's room. Birth certificates put him there if his was never amended and put him in the men's room if it was, on the basis of paperwork. Each rule places someone where its proposer would object to seeing them. That happens because the rules track presentation and role, and those are what a transitioned person has changed.

You can hear it in how the words are used. When someone says that woman is beautiful, they are not saying that organism whose reproductive system is organised around egg production is aesthetically pleasing. When a father says my girl is bright, he is not reporting a gamete finding. No one has ever used these words that way in speech. The everyday words track social reality. The biological terms track reproductive organisation. They coincide for nearly everyone, which is why it is easy to treat them as one thing, and they come apart exactly where this argument lives.

8. On gender dysphoria

That covers the categories. The condition itself needs stating carefully, because it is where both sides overclaim.

Gender dysphoria is a diagnosis in DSM-5-TR. The ICD-11 lists gender incongruence and moved it out of the mental disorders chapter into conditions related to sexual health. No medical body disputes that the condition exists, that it produces clinically significant distress, or that it is not chosen. That last point is the one worth pressing on anyone who calls it a preference: they will not be able to explain why a person would prefer something that gets them disowned, assaulted and unemployed at rates nobody prefers.

What is not settled is the cause. The leading hypothesis is that gender identity is shaped by prenatal hormone exposure interacting with genetic factors, along a developmental trajectory that can diverge from the body's. It is supported by twin studies showing heritability, by brain imaging that finds some sex-atypical features in some regions, and by natural experiments like CAIS and 5-alpha-reductase deficiency. It is not proven. The imaging findings are population averages with small effects and sexual orientation is a confound in several of them, and anyone who tells you the neurobiology is established is ahead of the evidence.

It also does not need to be established. Medicine accommodated epilepsy for two thousand years before anyone knew what a seizure was. Most psychiatric conditions are diagnosed on symptoms and course without a known mechanism, and are treated anyway. The demand for a settled cause before a condition is taken seriously is not one applied to anything else.

9. Adults and adolescents are different questions

Everything on this site is about adults, and the definition on the definitions page says so. Medical treatment of adolescents is a separate question and it is legitimately contested. The systematic reviews commissioned for the Cass Review found the evidence on puberty blockers insufficient and inconsistent, and the evidence on hormones in under-18s suggestive but low quality, and several European health systems have restricted both to research settings. A person can accept every step on this page and still hold that adolescent medicine needs better evidence than it has.

The two questions get run together in both directions. The adult case is used to wave through the adolescent one, and the adolescent evidence is used to refuse the adult one. Neither move is honest. The adult evidence is a different body of work, the definition here is about adults, and nothing in the Cass Review addresses whether a diagnosed adult who has transitioned is a woman.

10. The definition this produces

Everything above rules things out rather than settling anything. It shows that the strict biological definition fails on people it was never built to sort, that the failures run in both directions, and that failing it does not tell anyone how a person should be treated.

The definition that comes out of it is set out in full on its own page.

It requires a diagnosis and medical transition, undertaken or intended. It is not self-identification, and it is not the definition you started with.
