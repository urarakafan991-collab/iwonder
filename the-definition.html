If you believe sex determines gender

Taking the gamete definition seriously and applying it consistently.

A walk through your own definitions and what they actually require of you.

Assuming your belief is:

A woman is an adult female person. A man is an adult male person.

Female means someone whose internal reproductive system is arranged around the production of eggs. Male means someone whose internal reproductive system is arranged around the production of sperm.

Take that definition seriously and apply it consistently. This is what it produces.

1. What the definition produces

Under this definition, a woman with Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome (CAIS) is a man. She was born with a vagina. She has no male sex characteristics aside from internal testicles. But her reproductive system revolves around the production of sperm. The common argument is that it's what the system is arranged around, not necessarily what it produces.

If biological sex is directly correlated with gender, then you're telling women with this condition that they are men for the exact same reasons you say trans women are men.

You cannot exclude someone for not fulfilling the criteria while simultaneously accepting someone else who also doesn't fulfill it.

If you recognize women with CAIS as women, despite holding the definition above, this applies to you:

Then you're saying there are exceptions to a definition you said had no exceptions. So are you going to admit that the definition isn't ideal, and that it doesn't properly serve human beings in everyday society? Looking at a species purely at the biological level, with no social factors involved, it sorts well. That's what it was built for. But using it to sort people socially, it starts to fall apart and fail some of them. The people it fails are people with biological conditions like CAIS, and others like it, and people with neurobiological conditions, like gender dysphoria.

If you recognize women with CAIS as men:

Then that shouldn't have any bearing on their life. They will continue to live as women, be treated as women, and navigate the world as women. Your classification changes nothing about their reality. Just as it shouldn't have any bearing on the life of a trans woman.

Either way, the outcome is the same. The strict biological definition of sex describes internal organization. It does not, on its own, determine who someone is in the world or how they should be treated.

2. It works in the other direction too

CAIS shows the definition failing in one direction. The same failure happens in reverse.

Persistent Müllerian Duct Syndrome (PMDS) is a condition in which a 46,XY individual, biologically male by the gamete definition, is born with a uterus and fallopian tubes because their body didn't respond to Anti-Müllerian Hormone properly. Most people with PMDS identify and live as men.

The retained uterus in PMDS is typically rudimentary, but some documented cases involve enough function to cause recurring cyclic bleeding.

Under the strict binary: these are men with a uterus, sometimes one that sheds its lining. Another thing that "doesn't happen" under the framework, happening.

3. The inconsistency both cases expose

Put the two side by side. A person with CAIS is biologically male by the gamete definition, but is born with a vagina and female external anatomy, and lives as a woman. A person with PMDS is biologically male by the gamete definition, but is born with a uterus.

The two break the framework in different places. A woman with CAIS meets the "reproductive system organized around sperm production" test just as fully as a trans woman does, and almost nobody calls her a man. A man with PMDS is called a man by everyone, and he has a uterus his own definition says he shouldn't.

That's the contradiction, and it runs in both directions. If the sperm-organization criterion actually determined the answer, it would return the same answer for everyone who meets it. A woman with CAIS meets it. A trans woman meets it. The criterion returns "woman" for one and "man" for the other. A rule that gives different outputs for identical inputs isn't the rule being applied. Something else is producing the answer, and the criterion is being named afterward.

The same holds for the traits. If having a uterus is what makes someone a woman, then a man with PMDS would be a woman. He isn't, and nobody says he is. So that isn't the criterion either.

4. Typical traits are not definitional requirements

If you've tried to use the argument "human beings have two legs, so does a person without two legs mean human beings don't have two legs?", here is the response to that.

In human biology, we never state that human beings strictly have two legs. The biological rule is that human beings typically have two legs.

Assuming your belief is that a woman is strictly someone whose reproductive biology revolves around the production of eggs, and a man is strictly someone whose reproductive biology revolves around the production of sperm, with no exceptions.

If we apply that same strictness to the two-legs trait, a human being without two legs is not a human being. That's what the strict version produces.

I don't object to the typical version. You can tell me that a woman typically has reproductive biology that revolves around the production of eggs, and that a man typically has reproductive biology that revolves around the production of sperm, and I don't object to either of those. I object to the "strictly," in exactly the same way I object to it in "human beings strictly have two legs." Women typically have this. Not all do. Men typically have this. Not all do.

You could respond that this isn't a typical trait at all, that it's a required one. But that means you're telling a woman with an intersex condition that she is a man. And as already established, you can't say trans women are men for meeting these criteria and then turn around and say an intersex woman isn't a man despite meeting the exact same criteria you say make someone one.

So there are two options. Either she is a man, because she doesn't meet the strict requirement, which means you're telling intersex women they are men. Or this isn't the strict criterion you've been arguing it is, because it doesn't hold for some people.

5. Why the definition behaves this way

None of this means the biological definition is worthless. It means people are asking it to do a job it was never built for.

What tends to be forgotten about the definition of sex is that it's a heuristic category. It attempts to sort as many people as it can into the least number of categories. That's why the definition has historically changed whenever new findings emerge. Just like how other areas of science are never considered settled, only ranked higher depending on how much evidence supports them, the same is true for biology.

A category that does not adjust for the thousands of biological variations caused by disorders of sex development or any sex-related condition isn't necessarily a flawed category. But it is a category that should only be used when describing what a person is internally, not who they are socially or how they are treated socially.

The definition tells you about internal reproductive organization. It does not tell you who someone is socially or how they should be treated.

6. Why the definition doesn't generate social rules

Every social rule built around sex is actually tracking something downstream of the definition, not the definition itself. Why do men and women use different bathrooms? Not because of gametes. Because of secondary sex characteristics, body composition, and social norms. Why separate sports? Not chromosomes. Hormones, muscle mass, skeletal structure.

The definition points at internal reproductive organization, but the social world responds to everything else: how someone looks, how they present, how they move through daily life. When all of those downstream traits align with the definition, the category works seamlessly. When they don't, the definition alone can't tell you what to do. That's what makes it a heuristic.

You can see this in how the words actually function. When someone says "that woman is beautiful," they're not saying "that organism whose internal reproductive system is organized around egg production is aesthetically pleasing." When someone says "your girl is beautiful" about a daughter, they're not making a statement about gamete organization. Nobody has ever used these words that way. Every everyday use of "woman," "man," "girl," and "boy" is tracking social reality, not biology. The biological definition is a scientific tool. The social word is what people actually speak. They overlap almost entirely, but they're not the same thing, and pretending they are is the mistake being made.

7. On gender dysphoria

That covers the categories. Now the condition itself.

People often hear "hypothesis" and assume it means a guess. In science it doesn't. A regular hypothesis can be little more than a guess. A scientific hypothesis is an explanation built from actual evidence and tested against research, which is why it carries weight even before it's fully settled.

The leading scientific hypothesis for gender dysphoria is that gender identity develops through prenatal hormone exposure interacting with genetic factors, during fetal development, at different stages than the body does. If the hormonal environment shifts between these windows, the brain and body can develop along different pathways. This is where the overwhelming majority of evidence currently points.

This is supported by twin studies, prenatal hormone research, and neurological studies. Gender identity has documented neurobiological correlates. Gender dysphoria is not "being emotionally distressed about being the wrong gender." It is a diagnosable condition. It is not a choice.

There is also a condition that shows the same mechanism working in plain sight. 5-alpha reductase deficiency is a genetic condition in which a 46,XY individual can't convert testosterone into dihydrotestosterone properly. Their internal male reproductive structures develop normally, but their external genitalia often appear predominantly female at birth, so many are assigned female and raised as girls. At puberty, a surge in testosterone triggers virilization, and many of those individuals raised as girls experience a shift in gender identity toward male during or after puberty.

That is a documented case of gender identity changing, and the thing that changed it was hormones. If identity were simply a preference someone adopts, a hormonal event should have no bearing on it. It does. This is the same mechanism the prenatal hormone hypothesis describes, showing up later in life where it can actually be observed.

Transition is the medical treatment, and social integration is part of why it works. "Just treat it medically" is not a counter. Transition is the medical treatment.

8. The definitions this actually produces

Put all of the above together and this is what a workable definition looks like.

A woman is an adult human person who is either biologically female and identifies with the female category, or someone who has a condition with biological or neurobiological traits, such as Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome or gender dysphoria, that results in them living as female.

A man is an adult human person who is either biologically male and identifies with the male category, or someone who has a condition with biological or neurobiological traits, such as gender dysphoria, that results in them living as male.

A girl is a young human person who is either biologically female and identifies with the female category, or someone who has a condition with biological or neurobiological traits, such as Complete Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome or gender dysphoria, that results in them living as female.

A boy is a young human person who is either biologically male and identifies with the male category, or someone who has a condition with biological or neurobiological traits, such as gender dysphoria, that results in them living as male.

Or, without the diagnostic requirement:

A woman is an adult human person who identifies with the female category, regardless of their biological sex.

A man is an adult human person who identifies with the male category, regardless of their biological sex.

A girl is a young human person who identifies with the female category, regardless of their biological sex.

A boy is a young human person who identifies with the male category, regardless of their biological sex.

These definitions are not circular. "Woman" and "man" are defined in terms of "female" and "male," which refer to the gamete-organization classification. The second clause in the first set covers people with conditions, whether developmental or neurobiological, that result in them living as the other sex.

The phrase "identifies with the female/male category" exists to prevent a logical leak: without it, a trans man (biologically female, living as male) would technically qualify as a woman under the first clause. The identification clause ensures that people who have transitioned away from their birth sex aren't captured by a definition they've moved beyond.

The first set is a transmedicalist framework. It scopes the exception to people with a diagnosed condition who take steps to medically transition, not to self-identification alone. It has a clear, externally checkable boundary, but it excludes people who are trans without a diagnosis, without access to treatment, or who don't want medical transition. The second set covers those people and doesn't tie the category to healthcare access, but it gives up the diagnostic anchor that the sections above lean on. Both accept everything argued before this point. The disagreement between them isn't about whether the strict biological definition fails. It's about how wide the resulting category should be.
