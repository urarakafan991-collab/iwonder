Which claims actually hold up

Three lists: what the research supports, what gets overstated on this side, and what is genuinely open.

Every other page on this site tests the other side's claims. This one tests its own, because a page that only audits its opponents isn't an audit.

Three lists: what the research actually supports, what gets overstated by people arguing this side, and what is genuinely unsettled.

1. Solid ground

The historical record. Not in dispute by anybody. Every legal right women hold was previously withheld, argued for against organised opposition, and then granted. This is documentary history, not interpretation.

The raw pay gap exists. Around 82 cents to the dollar at the US median. What it measures is contested. That it exists is not.

The within-occupation gap is real. Goldin's finding that roughly 68% of the gap would close by equalising pay inside occupations is the strongest single result in this area, and it survives the obvious objection that women simply pick lower-paying fields.

The motherhood penalty. It shows up cleanly in longitudinal data: matched men and women earning the same, diverging after a first child, not converging afterwards. This is about as clean as social science gets, because the comparison is between the same people before and after.

Unpaid domestic work falls disproportionately on women. Time-use surveys, consistent across countries and decades.

2. Overstated, and it costs you the argument

“Women earn 77 cents for the same work.” The figure is a raw median across all jobs and all hours. Attaching “for the same work” to it is false, and it is the single most common own goal in this debate. It hands any informed opponent an immediate, correct win, and once they've had it they stop listening to the parts that are true.

Any pay figure quoted without saying whether it's raw or adjusted. The two answer different questions. A great deal of arguing about the pay gap is two people answering different questions at each other and concluding the other is lying.

“The remaining gap is discrimination.” Nobody has shown this. What's left after adjustment is a residual, which means everything not captured by the variables in the model. Some of it is probably discrimination. Calling all of it discrimination is an assumption wearing a number's clothes.

Statistics that have come loose from their source. Figures circulate on this side of the argument with the scope, year and definition stripped off, the same way they do on the other side. A statistic you can't trace is a statistic you should stop repeating, regardless of which conclusion it supports.

Every item in this section is a place where overclaiming loses an argument that the honest version wins. That's the only reason to be strict about it.

3. Genuinely unsettled

How much of the residual gap is discrimination. Competent economists disagree, using the same data, in good faith.

Whether occupational sorting is itself downstream of discrimination. This is the hinge the whole pay gap argument turns on, and it is not resolved. If women choose lower-paid fields freely, the adjusted figure is close to the truth. If those choices are shaped by what girls are told is available to them, the adjusted figure is an undercount by construction. There is evidence pointing both ways.

What should be done. Structural reform of how hours are rewarded, legal mandates, transparency requirements, or nothing at all. This is a values question wearing an empirical costume, and the evidence constrains it without deciding it.

A page claiming all three of these were settled would be easier to write and far easier to knock down.

The reason the first list is worth taking seriously is that this page was willing to write the second and third.
