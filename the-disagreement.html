Where the real disagreement sits

Once the definitional argument is out of the way, what is actually left.

If you have read the other pages, you and this site agree on more than either of us expected. Sex is binary. Trans women are male by the gamete definition. Intersex conditions are variations within the two sexes. Gender dysphoria is a real diagnosis and is not chosen. Its cause is not settled. The evidence for treating adolescents is weaker than advocates claim. We already call the woman with CAIS a woman without rewriting a single textbook. All of that is common ground, and it is more ground than most of these arguments ever reach.

What remains is narrow and empirical. In which specific settings, if any, should males who have medically transitioned be included in female categories, and on what criteria? Elite sport, where the answer turns on measurements of the body and may well be exclusion. Prisons, where it turns on risk and on data that no country currently collects well. Medical records, where sex and gender both need recording because they matter for different things. Those are policy questions. They are answered by evidence about effects in those settings, and the evidence is thin enough that both sides currently argue from anecdote.

Two things follow from the narrowness.

The first is that none of those questions requires a verdict on what a person is called. You can hold that trans women should not run in the women's 100 metres and call them women. You can hold that prison placement should be assessed case by case on risk and call them women. Most people who have thought about sport already do this without noticing. The policy questions and the naming question were never the same question, and the argument only felt like one because the naming was being used as a proxy for the policy.

The second is a test. Look at what is actually being demanded, and compare it with what the empirical questions could justify. Bans on care for adults, in jurisdictions that have passed them, are not justified by any finding about sport. Bathroom rules that apply to a post-operative woman who has lived as one for thirty years are not justified by any finding about prisons. Refusing a name and a pronoun costs nothing and settles no policy question at all. When the demands run far past what the evidence could support, the evidence was not the motive. It was the cover.

So here is where it ends. If you accept everything above and still refuse the courtesy of a name, the disagreement was never about biology, or safeguarding, or sport. It was about whether you think courtesy is owed to a person with a condition they did not choose, who treated it the only way it can be treated. That is a values question, not a scientific one, and it deserves to be argued as one.

Say that instead.
